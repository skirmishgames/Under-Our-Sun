Help: blocks, formatting, icons and patterns

How a mission is built

A mission is a short deck of numbered parts. Players read part 1 at setup and each part tells them when to read the next one. The editor starts every new mission from the community mission template: briefing, setup, special rules, mission goal and objectives on part 1, story on the middle parts, success and failure on the last part.

Empty blocks are left off the card, so you can keep the template blocks you do not need without them printing.

Block types

  • Section with heading: a panel with a heading such as BRIEFING, SETUP or SPECIAL RULES.
  • Story text: narrative with no heading.
  • Mission goal, Objectives, Bonus objective, Success, Failure: centred text under a coloured label.
  • Roguelike reward: the extra reward for roguelike mode, under its own label. Use {star} for points.
  • Tip: a short hint, printed with "Tip:" in front.
  • Picture: a map setup diagram or similar, with an optional caption.

Patterns and branching parts

  • The Insert pattern menu in each block adds wording for setups the official missions use: tile stacks, deck changes, spawns, clocks, objective tokens and more. Replace the parts in [brackets].
  • For a branching mission, type a label such as 6 A in a part's label box. The card shows PART 6 A/6, and parts that share a number count once.

Item names

The "Insert item" list in each block holds every craftable item and piece of gear from the crafting overview, grouped as tools, gear, healing and weapons. It adds the name in bold, for example Binoculars, so setup and reward text matches the cards in the box. Items marked CP01 need that set.

Icons

Text that does not fit

Text sits at the bottom of the card and the art shows above it. When a part gets long, the font shrinks a little to fit. If it still does not fit, the editor marks the part in red. Split it into another part or trim it.

Designing a mission

This guide comes from taking apart all 14 official missions (base game and Content Pack 01), the community mission template, fan missions and the designer's answers on BoardGameGeek. The numbers in brackets point to missions in the Mission index that use each idea.

Spark generator

Roll until something makes you curious. "Start a mission from this" opens a new mission with the spark saved in its author notes.

What every official mission shares

  • One to six parts. Competitive missions use one or two cards. Cooperative missions use two to six, most often four to six.
  • Brief info on the back of part 1: a one-line hook, the playing time and the player count, with "(competitive)" where it applies.
  • A briefing first. Usually the morning meeting in New Haven, with a veteran or scout speaking in quotes. Several part 1 cards hold only the briefing and send players straight to part 2, so setup stays hidden until it is needed (2, 4, 14).
  • Every part ends with a trigger: "Once X happens, read Mission N - Part Y."
  • Success and failure share the last card. Content Pack 01 adds a roguelike reward line to the success text.

1. Start with one sentence

Write the mission in one sentence: who needs what, and why today. "New Haven's solar panels are wrecked and the fuel runs out in four days" (10) gives a goal, a place and a clock. If you cannot say it in one sentence, the mission is probably two missions.

  • What does New Haven lose if the characters fail?
  • Why can't they wait until tomorrow?
  • What will players remember a week later?
  • Cooperative, competitive, or both?

3. Build the map

The official missions build the map in five ways. The editor's "Insert pattern" menu has wording for each.

  • Hidden target in a stack (1, 2, 13, 14). Stack 1 is a few random tiles; stack 2 is random tiles shuffled with the target. Place stack 1 on top. With 5 tiles plus 5 and the target, the target turns up between the 6th and 11th tile explored. Mission 1 uses three stacks for two targets. Mission 14 hides New Haven itself in the stack, because the group starts far away.
  • Fixed layout from a diagram (3, 4, 5, 9, 10, 12, and part 4 of 7). Name the tile numbers, remove them from the stack, and show the layout with a picture block. Most Content Pack 01 missions do this. It suits arenas, routes and short missions.
  • Curated stacks (8). Put named tiles on top of the stack, or alternate two hand-picked stacks, to control what turns up without fixing the layout.
  • Grow the map mid-mission (4, 7). A later part adds tiles, for example a line of face-down tiles from where something happened, or approach lanes for enemies.
  • Leave it alone (6). Normal exploration, with the pressure coming from something else.

The box has 34 map tiles. New Haven is S01 and the target tiles carry an S number. Count every tile your setup names.

4. Use the decks

Rebuilding a deck changes a mission's feel with almost no new rules.

  • Hide a card at a depth: stack the enemy deck so the wanted enemy appears no earlier than the fourth draw (6).
  • A small custom deck: eight chosen enemy cards make a tightly themed mission (8).
  • Remove enemy types that would break the story (7).
  • A harsher night: build the night deck from enemy cards only (2).
  • A target inside the event deck: the thing you hunt turns up as an event (8).
  • Seed the loot deck with target cards for a treasure hunt (12).

5. Add a clock

  • Deadline: "be back before the morning of day 5" (10), "by tomorrow morning" (3, 4). Timed missions never go past day 5.
  • Hold out: survive a number of day phases (4).
  • Escalating penalty: infection from day 2, damage from day 5 (6). The clock gets louder instead of ending the game.
  • A changed day: no nights and a frozen day tracker for a mission set inside New Haven (8).
  • A pursuer or fleeing target that moves every phase is a clock players can see (2).

6. Place enemies with care

  • Guards on the target: revealing the target tile spawns named enemies there (1). Enemies on target tiles do not move in the preparation phase.
  • Waves: spawn enemies equal to the number of players each phase and roll a d8 for the lane (4).
  • Spawn rolls on movement: roll a die each time something enters a new tile. Use a harder die with more players (10, 11).
  • Bosses: give HP as a base plus 1 per player (8). Guards can stay neutral until attacked (8).
  • Exceptions: when an enemy breaks a normal rule, say so on the card (4). The rulebook wins over anything left unsaid.

7. Give players something to do besides fight

  • Objective tokens carried in an item slot, which can be dropped and picked up (6, 10, 13).
  • Required items that gate the next step: a light source (2), a rope (7), a tool (10).
  • Mission-only crafting for weapons, repairs or a special tool (4, 10), or a mission item with a cost (8).
  • Captives and interrogation: beat an enemy down to 1 or 2 HP instead of killing it (7, 8).
  • Barricades tracked with HP cubes on tile borders, or a pile of resources to clear (4, 11).
  • Allies and vehicles with their own HP and simple written behaviour (11).
  • Skill tasks: spend AP and pass 2 of 3 checks (10).
  • Two ways to win so groups can pick a plan (6, 10).

8. Scale with player count

The official missions scale with simple formulas, not separate setups:

  • enemies per wave equal to the number of players (4)
  • boss HP 5 plus 1 per player (8)
  • items needed: players plus 2 (6)
  • AP cost equal to the number of players (10)
  • an extra enemy, or a harder spawn die, at 4 players (8, 11)

9. Branches and choices

  • Route choice: on foot or by vehicle (7).
  • Method choice: brute force or craft the right tool (10).
  • The ending depends on how you got there: part 6 A if no fight happened, 6 B if one did (7); different success text if the ally survives (11).
  • Bonus objective with a reward (13).

For branching parts, type a label such as "6 A" in the part's label box. The card shows PART 6 A/6 and the total counts 6 A and 6 B once.

10. Competitive missions

Four official missions pit players against each other: score the most loot (3), last one standing (5), hold the hills (9) and first to find the stash (12). They share a few habits: one or two cards, a fixed arena around New Haven, starting equipment set by the card instead of bought, and survival upkeep switched off when it would slow the fight (9). Set the game mode to Competitive and the brief info adds "(competitive)".

11. Writing the cards

  • Write to the players as "you". Put quoted speech in italics.
  • Headings the official cards use: BRIEFING or INTRODUCTION, SETUP, SPECIAL RULES or MISSION RULES, SPECIAL ABILITY, MISSION GOAL, OBJECTIVES, SUCCESS, FAILURE. Story panels often get their own name, such as "A first lead" or "The journey back".
  • Bold what players act on: tile numbers, enemy names, item names, the part to read next.
  • A Tip block helps with tricky missions ("Try to sleep during the day").
  • Put each part's setup on that part's card, so players do not see what is coming.
  • Give failure a clear trigger too: all characters dead, a deadline passed, a barricade broken.

12. Plan for the traitor

Semi-cooperative play is recommended for 3 or more players. The three base game SoL cards ask the traitor to stop the mission goal, to collect 4 food and escape off a map edge, or to kill a character and burn a survivor's camp. Missions with something to carry, plenty of food or forced camping give the traitor room. Missions built around set encounters (4, 8, 11) work best fully cooperative.

13. Roguelike rewards

In roguelike mode each character earns 2 points for a completed mission. Content Pack 01 missions add a small group reward on top: 1 extra point plus one item, such as an A.E.D., a pistol, a rifle or a distillery (8, 10, 11, 13). Keep rewards that size, and use the Roguelike reward block. The Survivor log tracks points between missions.

14. Use the world

  • New Haven is one of the few free settlements, always short of water. Its outposts are vulnerable (4).
  • Lawrence runs the trading post. Merchants roam the wastes and can be traded with, robbed or attacked.
  • The Sons of Liberty rule protection zones under the mysterious Mr. Liberty and ban technology. Their camps, trucks and meeting points make good targets (7, 13).
  • The Prime Elite were the private army of the rich enclaves. Their aircraft and gear still turn up (14), and one of them might even be an ally (11).
  • Crawlers live in tunnels and hunt at night (2). Mutated animals roam the open desert.
  • Solar storms wreck electrical gear (10). Water and medicine are always scarce (6).
  • The ruins: collapsed highways, rail lines, gas stations, gutted high-rises on the horizon.

15. Playtest checklist

  • Play it solo with two characters at least once before sharing it.
  • Count components: every token, standee, tile and card your setup names must be in the box, or say which expansion it needs.
  • Use the game's terms: map tile, target map tile, spawn, initial combat round, preparation phase.
  • Check every part has a trigger to the next, and that failure can happen.
  • Check the mission at 2 and at 4 players. If it breaks, add a player-count formula.
  • Read the briefing aloud. If it takes more than a minute, trim it.
  • Set the difficulty marks honestly: one ! for easy, two for challenging, three for hard.
  • Write your playtest results in the author notes, then export the mission and share it.
